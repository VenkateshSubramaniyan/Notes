Each Nakshatra spans 13°20′ and contains four Padas of 3°20′ each. Gana follows the common Deva, Manushya and Rakshasa classification; Nakshatra Lord is the Vimshottari dasha ruler. Nakshatras that cross a Rashi boundary appear under both Rashis; the Rashi cell is merged across its listed rows. The overview ruler uses actual degrees; each Nakshatra spans 13°20′ in total and each Pada spans 3°20′.

Tithi, Nakshatra and Rashi — one lunar cycle

These are three different measurements, not three divisions of the same thing. Tithi measures the angular separation of the Sun and Moon; Nakshatra and Rashi locate the Moon against the sidereal zodiac.

SystemWhat it measuresOne unitFull-circle count
Tithi (திதி)Moon–Sun angular separation12°30 Tithis = 360°
Nakshatra (நட்சத்திரம்)Moon's sidereal longitude13°20′27 Nakshatras = 360°
Pada (பாதம்)Quarter of a Nakshatra3°20′108 Padas = 360°
Rashi (ராசி)30° zodiac sign occupied by Moon30°12 Rashis = 360°

Paksha and Tithi numbers

  • Shukla Paksha (வளர்பிறை): Pratipada to Pournami — Tithis 1–15; Sun–Moon separation increases from 0° towards 180°.
  • Krishna Paksha (தேய்பிறை): Pratipada to Amavasya — Tithis 16–30 in continuous numbering (or 1–15 when numbered within the Paksha); separation decreases from 180° towards 360°/0°.
  • Each Tithi spans 12° of elongation. Tithi boundaries are exact multiples of 12°; a Tithi's civil-day duration varies.

How the numbers fit

27 × 13°20′ = 360°; 12 × 30° = 360°; 108 × 3°20′ = 360°; and 30 × 12° = 360° for the Sun–Moon angular separation. Each Rashi contains exactly 9 Padas, or 2¼ Nakshatras.

The Moon takes about 27.3 days to complete one sidereal circuit relative to the stars, but about 29.5 days to complete a phase cycle from one New Moon to the next. During that synodic month it travels roughly one full circuit of the zodiac. Tithi is not fixed to a particular Nakshatra or Rashi: a Tithi can start and end while the Moon remains in one Nakshatra, or the Moon can cross a Nakshatra/Rashi boundary during a Tithi.

Abhijit in the Valmiki Ramayana

Abhijit is mentioned by name in the Kishkindha Kanda, when the monkey leaders set out to search for Sita. The verse says they travelled in the direction indicated by Abhijit:

अथ पवनसमानविक्रमाः प्लवगवराः प्रतिलब्धपौरुषाः ।
अभिजिदभिमुखां दिशं ययुर्जनकसुता परिमार्गणोन्मुखाः ॥

Meaning: The foremost monkeys, having regained their strength and eager to search for Janaka's daughter, proceeded towards the direction marked by Abhijit.

Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda 4.62.15 in some editions; numbered 4.63.15 in others. Here Abhijit is used as a celestial direction/constellation marker, commonly interpreted as indicating the north.

Important distinction about Rama's birth: The Valmiki Ramayana's Bala Kanda birth account associates Rama's birth with Punarvasu (under the regency of Aditi), not Abhijit. Claims that Rama was born under Abhijit occur in some later traditional or astrological accounts; they should not be presented as the wording of Valmiki's birth passage.

References: Valmiki Ramayana 4.62.15 — Sanskrit text and translation · Bala Kanda, Sarga 18 — Rama's birth account.

Earth's axis slowly changes direction in a cycle called axial precession (about 26,000 years). Therefore the North Star changes over long periods. Polaris is close to the north celestial pole in our era; Vega was near the pole roughly 12,000–13,000 years ago and will again be a pole star in the distant future. Neither star defines the Tithi count.

Keep the distinction clear: 27 Nakshatras and 12 Rashis are divisions along the ecliptic (the Sun's apparent annual path); Polaris lies near the celestial pole, far from that belt. Vega is an actual star associated traditionally with Abhijit, but the Nakshatra divisions are measured as angular sectors, not by the physical size of stars.
